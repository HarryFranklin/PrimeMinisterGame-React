Once you have Life Satisfaction scores for a whole population, a single average only tells part of the story. Zoom in on the full distribution of scores and very different pictures start to emerge — pictures that matter enormously for policy. Here's what that data can actually tell you.

### The Mean (Average)
The mean is the sum of all Life Satisfaction scores divided by the population size. It answers: *"How happy is the typical citizen, overall?"* But a high mean can hide serious problems — a small wealthy group scoring very high can offset a large struggling group scoring very low.

Two towns can share an identical mean of 6 and still look nothing alike. In Town A, almost everyone scores between 5 and 7 — a broad, stable middle. In Town B, half the population scores 9 and the other half scores 3. Same average, two very different societies.

### The Floor (Minimum)
The floor is the lowest Life Satisfaction score present in the population — the worst-off citizens. It answers: *"How badly off are the most vulnerable members of society?"* Two societies can share the same mean while having very different floors.

<DistributionFloorDiagram />

### Dispersion (Inequality)
Dispersion measures how spread out scores are across the population. Consider two societies both averaging 6 out of 10. In one, almost everyone clusters between 5 and 7 — low dispersion. In the other, half the population scores 9 and half scores 3 — high dispersion, even though the mean is identical. High dispersion means some citizens are thriving while others are struggling, even if the headline number looks fine.

<DistributionDispersionDiagram />

### Why This Matters
As you'll see over the next few pages, different policy frameworks care about different properties of a distribution. Some frameworks fixate on the mean. Others fixate on the floor. Understanding what you're looking at is the first step to evaluating whether a policy is actually working.
