Before we can talk about aggregating Life Satisfaction, it helps to know how it's actually collected. Every citizen answers the same 0-10 survey question — *"Overall, how satisfied are you with your life nowadays?"* — and a government ends up with millions of individual responses, not one number.

Once you have those scores for a whole population, a single average only tells part of the story. Look at the full spread of responses and very different pictures start to emerge — pictures that matter enormously for policy. Here's what that data can actually tell you.

### The Mean (Average)
The mean is the sum of all Life Satisfaction scores divided by the population size. It answers: *"How happy is the typical citizen, overall?"* But a high mean can hide serious problems — a small wealthy group scoring very high can offset a large struggling group scoring very low. A high mean, therefore, does not necessarily mean prosperity.

Two towns can share an identical mean of 5.5 and still look nothing alike. In Town A, almost everyone scores between 4 and 7 — a broad, stable middle. In Town B, the population is sharply divided, with large groups scoring either a 3 or an 8. Same average, two very different societies.

### The Floor (Minimum)
The floor is the lowest Life Satisfaction score present in the population — the worst-off citizens. It answers: *"How badly off are the most vulnerable members of society?"* 

Two societies can share the same mean while having very different floors. For example, two societies might both average 5.0, but Society A leaves its most vulnerable citizens at a score of 2, while Society B secures a floor of 4.

### Dispersion (Inequality)
Consider two societies both averaging 5.0 out of 10. In one, almost everyone clusters tightly between 4 and 6 — low dispersion. In the other, scores are spread completely evenly from 0 to 10 — high dispersion, even though the mean is identical.

### Why This Matters
As you'll see over the next few pages, different policy frameworks care about different properties of a distribution. Some frameworks fixate on the mean. Others fixate on the floor. Understanding what you're looking at is the first step to evaluating whether a policy is actually working.
