Before governments can decide *what* to prioritise, they need to be able to read wellbeing data accurately. A distribution of Life Satisfaction scores across a population can be summarised in three distinct ways, each telling a different story.

### The Mean (Average)
The mean is the sum of all Life Satisfaction scores divided by the population size. It answers: *"How happy is the typical citizen, overall?"* But a high mean can hide serious problems — a small wealthy group scoring very high can offset a large struggling group scoring very low.

<DistributionMeanDiagram />

### The Floor (Minimum)
The floor is the lowest Life Satisfaction score present in the population — the worst-off citizens. It answers: *"How badly off are the most vulnerable members of society?"* Two societies can share the same mean while having very different floors.

<DistributionFloorDiagram />

### Dispersion (Inequality)
Dispersion measures how spread out scores are across the population. A society with low dispersion has everyone clustered near the same score; high dispersion means some citizens are thriving while others are struggling, even if the mean looks fine.

<DistributionDispersionDiagram />

### Why This Matters
As you'll see over the next few pages, different policy frameworks care about different properties of a distribution. Some frameworks fixate on the mean. Others fixate on the floor. Understanding what you're looking at is the first step to evaluating whether a policy is actually working.
