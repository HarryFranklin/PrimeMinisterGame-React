A country's GDP per person is an average. It is the total amount the country produces, divided by the number of people. Like any average, it can look healthy while hiding how things are spread out. Two countries can have the same GDP per person and very different lives inside them.

Life Satisfaction has the same weakness if we only look at its average. Every citizen answers the survey question with a score from 0 to 10, so a government ends up with millions of individual scores, not just one number. Looking at the whole spread of scores shows what a single average cannot.

We sometimes group people by their score:

- **Struggling:** 0 to 3
- **Just getting by:** 4 to 5
- **Comfortable:** 6 to 8
- **Thriving:** 9 to 10

Three features of that spread matter most for policy: the mean (average), the floor (minimum) and the dispersion (spread).

### The Mean (Average)
The mean is found by adding up all the scores and dividing by the number of people. It answers the question: *"How happy is the typical citizen?"*

A high mean can hide serious problems, because a group with very high scores can pull the average up and cover for a large group with very low scores. So a high mean does not always mean that a country is doing well.

Take two towns that both have a mean of 5.5. In Town A, nearly everyone scores between 4 and 7, which is a broad and steady middle. In Town B, the population is sharply divided, with large groups scoring either 3 or 8. The average is the same, but the two towns are very different places to live.

### The Floor (Minimum)
The floor is the lowest score in the population, so it shows how the worst-off people are doing. It answers the question: *"How badly off are the most vulnerable people?"*

Two societies can have the same mean but very different floors. For example, both might average 5.0, but in Society A the most vulnerable citizens score 2, while in Society B the floor is 4.

### Dispersion (Inequality)
Dispersion is how spread out the scores are, and a wide spread means high inequality. A simple way to describe it is the range: the gap between the lowest and highest scores.

Picture two societies that both average 5.0. In the first, almost everyone scores between 4 and 6, so the range is about 2 points. That is low dispersion. In the second, scores are spread evenly from 0 to 10, so the range is 10 points. That is high dispersion. The mean is identical, but the second society is far more unequal.

### What Policies Can Change
Different policies, and different ways of choosing between policies, affect these three measures in different ways. One policy might raise the mean and leave the floor where it was. Another might lift the floor and barely move the mean. A third might narrow the spread. Knowing which measure a policy moves is the first step in judging whether it is working.
