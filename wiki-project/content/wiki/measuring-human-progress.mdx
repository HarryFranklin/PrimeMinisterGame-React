For decades, governments have judged how well a country is doing using economic measures. The most important one is **Gross Domestic Product (GDP)**, which adds up everything a country produces in a year, such as cars sold, houses built and hours worked.

GDP was designed to measure the size of the economy, not the wellbeing of the people who live in it.

*GDP tells you how fast the economic engine is running. It does not tell you if the journey is any good.*

A country's GDP can keep growing while the gap between rich and poor gets wider, while the environment is damaged and while communities fall apart. None of that shows up in the GDP figure, and GDP says nothing about *how the money is shared out*.

> **Side Note: What GDP Ignores**  
> Some things add almost nothing to GDP but still matter a lot to people. Less noise pollution and more community social care are two examples. A wellbeing measure can pick up the value of things like these, because it asks people how their lives are going.

That gap between "the economy is growing" and "people are doing well" is why many governments now look at **Subjective Wellbeing**. This measures how people say they feel about their own lives, so it puts people first rather than money.

### Defining Life Satisfaction
**Life Satisfaction** is one kind of Subjective Wellbeing, and it is the one most widely used in government statistics. It is the evaluation people make of their own life, when they weigh up what they have achieved against their own standards and hopes.

> **Side Note: Asking the Question**  
> Life Satisfaction is measured by asking: *"Overall, how satisfied are you with your life nowadays?"* People answer on a scale from 0 ("not at all satisfied") to 10 ("completely satisfied"). Everything else you will read builds on that 0-10 score.

In the UK, the average score is around 7 out of 10. But that single number hides a lot of variation between regions, age groups and income levels. The next page shows how to read what is hidden *inside an average*.
