For decades, policymakers have relied on a handful of economic metrics — Gross Domestic Product (GDP) chief among them, alongside measures like employment rate and growth in average income — to judge whether a country is on the right track. GDP totals up everything a country produces: cars sold, houses built, hours worked. However, it was designed to measure the size of the economy, not the wellbeing of the people living in it.

*GDP measures the speed of the economic engine — not the quality of the destination.*

A pure focus on economic growth says nothing about how the proceeds of that growth are shared out, or how people would actually choose to weigh those proceeds against everything else in their lives — questions much closer to how individuals make decisions about their own wellbeing (see [Personal Utility](/wiki/personal-utility)) than to how much a country produces. Growth can rise even as inequality widens, the environment degrades, and social relationships fray, none of which shows up in the GDP figure.

> **Side Note: The Origins of GDP**  
> Simon Kuznets, who helped develop early national income accounting in the 1930s, explicitly warned against conflating a measure of national income with the welfare of a nation.

That gap between "the economy is growing" and "people are doing well" is why modern policy is shifting toward **Subjective Wellbeing**—giving policymakers the tools necessary to prioritise human welfare over financial output.

### Defining Life Satisfaction
An example of Subjective Wellbeing in practice is **Life Satisfaction**: the measure used throughout this wiki, and increasingly in government policy. It's an evaluation individuals make about their own life and circumstances, weighing what they've achieved against their own personal standards and expectations.

> **Side Note: Asking the Question**  
> Life Satisfaction is typically measured with a single survey question: *"Overall, how satisfied are you with your life nowadays?"* — answered on a scale from 0 ("not at all satisfied") to 10 ("completely satisfied"). It's this simple 0-10 answer that everything else in this wiki builds on.

For reference, recent UK survey data puts average Life Satisfaction at somewhere around 7 out of 10 nationally — though that single number hides considerable variation by region, age, and income. Reading what's hidden inside an average like that is exactly what the next page covers.
