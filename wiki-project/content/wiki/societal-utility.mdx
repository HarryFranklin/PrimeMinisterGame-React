As the last page said, people think differently about their own outcomes and about other people's. This is the idea behind the veil of ignorance: choosing how society should work without knowing where you will end up in it. **Societal Utility** is the second kind of thinking: how people weigh wellbeing when they decide for a whole population, not just for themselves.

People take more care with other people's wellbeing than with their own. They are less willing to gamble with someone else's wellbeing, especially someone who is already worse off. This is called **inequality aversion**: disliking it when some people are left far behind. It is the societal counterpart of the risk aversion on the last page.

A government that follows this idea judges success partly by how fairly wellbeing is shared, and not only by growth. The measure it aims to raise is the **National Fairness Index (NFI)**.

> **Side Note: What Real People Think**  
> A UK study asked 300 adults to make choices about risks to themselves and about inequality in society. About 4 in 5 (79%) showed risk aversion in choices about their own lives. Nearly 9 in 10 (87%) showed inequality aversion in choices about society. Almost three quarters (73%) showed more inequality aversion than risk aversion. These views were not linked to how people voted. (These figures leave out choices involving death.)

Because of this, a policy can look good under Personal Utility, because most people gain. The same policy can look bad under Societal Utility, because the losses land on people who were already worse off. The next page shows what happens when the two disagree about the same policy. It also shows why the government's chosen approach decides the outcome.
