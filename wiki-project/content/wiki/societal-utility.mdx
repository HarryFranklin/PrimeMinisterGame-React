**Societal Utility** (also called social utility) measures how people value a gain in Life Satisfaction when they are deciding for *other people*. It comes from the gambles on the last page that were made for a group of citizens.

### Behind the Veil of Ignorance
In those gambles, you belong to the group, but you do not know whether you would be one of the people who rise or one of the people who fall. This is the **veil of ignorance** from [The Rawlsian Approach](/wiki/rawlsian-framework): choosing how society should work without knowing where you will end up in it.

When people choose like this, they tend to be cautious. They only back a policy that drops some people down if very few people would fall. In their eyes, a drop for someone else counts for more than an equal rise.

That caution shows up in the curve. It climbs very steeply at the bottom and is almost flat at the top, so lifting the worst-off counts for much more than adding to people who are already doing well.

A government that follows this idea judges each policy by how citizens would value the result if they were responsible for everyone else. The measure it aims to raise is the **National Fairness Index (NFI)**.

### The Case for Societal Utility
Governments make decisions on behalf of millions of people: how to share a budget, whose wellbeing to prioritise, and which group has to bear a cost. Societal Utility **reflects what people endorse when they are responsible for others**. If people are this careful when a choice is about other people's lives, perhaps a government, which is always choosing for others, should be careful too.

But people do not only make choices for others. They also make choices about their own lives, and there they choose differently. The next page looks at that.
