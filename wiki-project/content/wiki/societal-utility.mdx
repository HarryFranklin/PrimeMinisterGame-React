Where Personal Utility captures how someone weighs outcomes for themselves, **Societal Utility** captures how people weigh outcomes for *others* — the logic a government has to use whenever it makes a decision on behalf of the whole population.

The two turn out not to be the same curve scaled up. People are consistently more averse to inequality in others' outcomes than they are to risk in their own — less willing to gamble with someone else's wellbeing, especially someone worse-off, than they are with their own.

| Life Satisfaction | Personal Utility | Societal Utility |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 0.00 | 0.00 |
| 4 | 0.55 | 0.72 |
| 6 | 0.78 | 0.87 |
| 8 | 0.91 | 0.95 |
| 10 | 1.00 | 1.00 |

Look at the gap between the two columns. Lifting someone from 2 to 4 is worth 0.55 of the personal utility range, but 0.72 of the societal one — society credits that same real-world improvement more heavily than the individual's own risk calculus would. By the time you reach the top of the scale, the gap has almost closed: going from 8 to 10 is worth about the same either way. In other words, Personal Utility and Societal Utility mostly agree about gains near the top — they diverge sharply at the bottom, which is exactly where **inequality aversion** does its work.

> **Side Note: Eliciting Public Preferences**  
> Research consistently finds that the public penalises inequality directly, not just indirectly through concern for the worst-off. When asked to evaluate policy, people typically say that moving someone out of deprivation matters more than increasing the wealth of those already comfortable — even when the two changes are numerically identical.

Because of this, a policy can look positive under Personal Utility — most people gain — while looking negative under Societal Utility, because the losses are concentrated among people who were already worse off. Evaluating policy by how the gains and losses are *distributed*, not just their total, is what Societal Utility is for.
