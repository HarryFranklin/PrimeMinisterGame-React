As discussed on the previous page, people reason differently about outcomes for themselves than they do for others whose position they don't fully know or share — the same intuition behind the veil of ignorance. **Societal Utility** captures that second kind of reasoning: the logic a government has to use whenever it makes a decision on behalf of the whole population, rather than for one person.

People are consistently more averse to inequality in others' outcomes than they are to risk in their own — less willing to gamble with someone else's wellbeing, especially someone worse-off, than they are with their own.

> **Side Note: Eliciting Public Preferences**  
> Research consistently finds that the public penalises inequality directly, not just indirectly through concern for the worst-off. When asked to evaluate policy, people typically say that moving someone out of deprivation matters more than increasing the wealth of those already comfortable — even when the two changes are numerically identical.

Because of this, a policy can look positive under Personal Utility — most people gain — while looking negative under Societal Utility, because the losses are concentrated among people who were already worse off. The next page shows exactly what happens when Personal Utility and Societal Utility disagree about the very same policy — and why, when they diverge, the government's chosen approach is what actually decides the outcome.
