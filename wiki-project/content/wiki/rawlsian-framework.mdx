In response to the failures of simple averages, the Rawlsian approach argues that a society should be judged by how it treats its most vulnerable citizens — not by how well it does on average.

> **Side Note: The Veil of Ignorance**  
> John Rawls proposed the "veil of ignorance" thought experiment: if you didn't know in advance where in society you would end up, you would rationally choose to design a system that prioritises the worst-off outcome — because you might be the one living it.

Rather than aiming to raise the average, this framework directs all political capital toward raising the **Minimum Wellbeing Baseline**: the Life Satisfaction score of the worst-off citizen — what the previous page called the *floor*. Policies are designed to protect the worst-off, even if doing so limits gains at the top of the distribution.

### The Limits of Strict Prioritisation
Rawlsian logic successfully guards against extreme deprivation, but taken to its strictest form it can become a straightjacket. If the only thing that matters is the floor, a policy that raises the worst-off citizen's Life Satisfaction by a fraction of a point should be chosen over one that leaves the worst-off exactly where they are but transforms life for almost everyone else. Most people's intuitions rebel at that trade-off once the gains on the other side get large enough — which suggests the floor can't be the *only* thing that matters, even to people who care most about the worst-off.

There's also a problem this framework shares with Benthamite Aggregation, even though it looks like the opposite approach: both treat every point on the 0-10 Life Satisfaction scale as worth exactly the same, wherever it falls. [We'll come back to that.](/wiki/issue-of-linearity)
