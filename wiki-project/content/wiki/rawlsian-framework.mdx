The Rawlsian approach takes a different view from simple averages. It argues that a society should be judged by how it treats its most vulnerable citizens, not by how well it does on average.

> **Side Note: The Veil of Ignorance**  
> The philosopher John Rawls asked people to imagine the following: you have to choose how society works, but you do not know where you will end up in it. You could be rich or poor, healthy or ill. Rawls argued you would choose a fair system that protects the worst-off, because that person could be you.

Instead of trying to raise the average, this approach puts all its political effort into raising the floor (minimum), which is the Life Satisfaction score of the worst-off person. We can call this the **Minimum Wellbeing Baseline (MWB)**. Rawlsian policies are designed to protect the worst-off, even if that limits the gains for people at the top.

### Why the Floor Isn't Everything
In its strictest form, the Rawlsian approach can become inflexible. Suppose one policy raises the worst-off person's score by a tiny fraction of a point, while another leaves them exactly where they are but transforms life for almost everyone else. If only the floor matters, the first policy must win. Most people find that hard to accept once the gains for everyone else become large enough, which suggests that the floor cannot be the *only* thing that matters, even to people who care most about the worst-off.

There is a deeper problem too. The Rawlsian approach looks at the raw 0-10 score of the worst-off person and tries to raise it, but it never asks how much a change is really worth to that person. The Benthamite approach has exactly the same blind spot. [We'll come back to that.](/wiki/mathematics-of-utility)

You will see both frameworks used on real policy over the next two pages. Then we return to the question they both leave out.
