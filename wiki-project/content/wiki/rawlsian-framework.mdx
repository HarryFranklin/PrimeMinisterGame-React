In response to the failures of simple averages, the Rawlsian approach argues that a society should be judged by how it treats its most vulnerable citizens — not by how well it does on average.

> **Side Note: The Veil of Ignorance**  
> John Rawls proposed the "veil of ignorance" thought experiment: if you didn't know in advance where in society you would end up, you would rationally choose to design a system that prioritises the worst-off outcome — because you might be the one living it.

Rather than aiming to raise the average, this framework directs all political capital toward raising the floor: the Life Satisfaction score of the worst-off citizen. Policies under Rawlsian logic are designed to protect the worst-off, even if doing so limits gains at the top of the distribution.

### Why the Floor Isn't Everything
Rawlsian logic successfully guards against extreme deprivation, but taken to its strictest form it can become inflexible. If the only thing that matters is the floor, a policy that raises the worst-off citizen's Life Satisfaction by a fraction of a point should be chosen over one that leaves the worst-off exactly where they are but transforms life for almost everyone else. Most people find that trade-off hard to accept once the gains on the other side get large enough — which suggests the floor, or more precisely the wellbeing of the worst-off, can't be the *only* thing that matters, even to people who care most about them.

There's a subtler problem too. Rawlsian logic looks at the worst-off citizen's raw Life Satisfaction number and tries to raise it — but it never asks how much a given change is actually worth to that person, or how anyone would weigh that change if they were the one deciding it for themselves versus deciding it on someone else's behalf. Neither Rawlsian nor Benthamite logic engages with that question at all. [We'll come back to that.](/wiki/issue-of-linearity)
