Everything so far has been about how to *judge* a policy's effect on wellbeing. But a government bureaucracy also has to decide, in advance, which policies are worth funding at all — and that means putting utility-weighted wellbeing logic into the same budgeting process used for every other government decision.

### Weighing Cost Against Wellbeing
To compare a prospective policy's financial cost against its expected effect on wellbeing, decision-makers need Life Satisfaction (or utility) changes expressed in the same terms as money. This is operationalised through the **WELLBY (Wellbeing-Adjusted Life Year)** — a one-point change in Life Satisfaction, on the 0-10 scale, for one person for one year — which can then be assigned a monetary value (e.g. via the HM Treasury Green Book) and dropped straight into a standard Cost-Benefit Analysis. In practice, policies are then ranked by the WELLBYs they're expected to produce per pound spent, the same way any other investment would be ranked by return.

> **Side Note: Pricing What GDP Ignores**  
> Once wellbeing is priced this way, it can capture the value of things GDP simply misses. A policy that reduces noise pollution or expands community social care may generate close to zero measured economic output, but can still be highly cost-effective once its WELLBY return is counted — because it's aimed squarely at the thing GDP was never designed to track in the first place.
