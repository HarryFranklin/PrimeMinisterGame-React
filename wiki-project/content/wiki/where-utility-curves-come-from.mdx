The last page showed a utility curve. But nobody can look inside someone's head and read off how much a point of Life Satisfaction is worth to them. So where does the curve come from?

Researchers ask people to make a series of choices and turn those choices into numbers. Each choice is between a *sure thing* and a *gamble*.

### Choosing Between Gambles
First, people read short descriptions of imagined lives, from very good to very hard, and give each one a Life Satisfaction score. Most people score these lives at about 10, 8, 6, 4 and 2.

Then they make choices like this one:

> **Example: A Choice for a Group of Citizens**  
> A large group of people all have a Life Satisfaction of 6. The government can fund one of two policies for them.  
> Policy A: everyone stays at 6. This outcome is certain.  
> Policy B: some people rise to 8, but others fall to 4. No one can tell beforehand who will benefit.  
> You belong to this group too, but you don't know whether you would be one of those who rise or one of those who fall.

The share of people who fall starts high, at 1 in 2. It then gets smaller step by step (1 in 5, 1 in 10, 1 in 100, 1 in 1,000, and so on) until the person is willing to choose Policy B. That switching point shows how much they fear the step down compared with how much they want the step up. If someone will only take the gamble when very few people would fall, the step down must matter far more to them than the step up.

Repeating this at different starting points (4, 6 and 8) traces out the whole curve, one step at a time.

### Two Versions of the Same Gamble
The same kind of gamble can be asked in two ways:

| | Choosing for others | Choosing for yourself |
|---|---|---|
| The question | Which policy would you fund for a group of citizens? | Which treatment would you choose for your own life? |
| Whose Life Satisfaction is at risk | Other people's (and maybe yours, but you don't know which group you would be in) | Your own |
| The curve it gives | **Societal Utility** | **Personal Utility** |

The version about your own life looks like this:

> **Example: A Choice About Your Own Life**  
> Imagine your Life Satisfaction is 6. You have had a health condition all your life, and your doctor says you must choose between two treatments.  
> Treatment A: you stay at 6. This outcome is certain.  
> Treatment B: if it works, you rise to 8. If it fails, you fall to 4.

Here it is the chance that the treatment fails that starts at 1 in 2 and gets smaller.

The curves on the next two pages are the averages of the answers given by about 300 UK adults. So a utility value is not a guess: it comes from the risks people would accept in gambles like these. The next page starts with the choices people make for others.
