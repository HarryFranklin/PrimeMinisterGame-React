The last three pages used two curves: one for Personal Utility and one for Societal Utility. But nobody can look inside someone's head and read off how much a point of Life Satisfaction is worth to them. So where do the curves come from?

Researchers ask people questions and turn their answers into numbers. The Personal and Societal Utility curves on the last three pages both come from one method: choosing between gambles. Societal Utility can also be measured a second way, by asking people directly. This page describes both.

### Measuring Both Curves: Choosing Between Gambles
In the first method, people make a series of choices. Each choice is between a *sure thing* and a *gamble*.

First, people read short descriptions of five imagined lives, from very good to very hard. They give each life a Life Satisfaction score. Most people score these lives at about 10, 8, 6, 4 and 2.

Then they make choices like this one:

> **Example: A Choice About Your Own Life**  
> Option A: you are certain to live the life scored 6.  
> Option B: you take a medical treatment. It will probably move you up to the life scored 8, but there is a 1 in 10 chance it moves you down to the life scored 4.

The chance of the bad outcome starts high, at 1 in 2. It then gets smaller and smaller (1 in 10, 1 in 100, and so on) until the person is willing to take the gamble. That switching point shows how much the person fears the step down compared with how much they want the step up. If someone will only gamble when the bad outcome is very unlikely, the step down must matter far more to them than the step up.

Choices about your own life give the **Personal Utility** curve. A second set of choices is about society:

> **Example: A Choice About Society**  
> You are a policymaker choosing between two policies.  
> Policy A: everyone ends up in the life scored 6.  
> Policy B: most people move up to the life scored 8, but some people move down to the life scored 4.  
> The policy affects you too, but you do not know which group you will be in.

Here, the share of people who lose out starts high and gets smaller, in the same way. These choices give the **Societal Utility** curve. The two curves you have seen on earlier pages are the averages of the answers given by about 300 UK adults.

### Another Way to Measure Societal Utility: Asking Directly
This method does not use gambles, and it only covers Societal Utility. People are simply asked how much each step up the scale matters.

First, people give their own Life Satisfaction score and are told how scores are spread across the UK. Then they are told that helping someone move from 2 to 3 is worth 100 units. For each step after that (3 to 4, 4 to 5, and so on up to 9 to 10), they move a dial from 0 to 100 to show how important it is to help someone make that step.

> **Example: A Direct Question**  
> Helping someone move from 2 to 3 is worth 100.  
> How important is it to help someone move from 8 to 9?  
> (0 = not at all important, 100 = very important)

This question is about helping *other people*, so it measures **Societal Utility**. It does not ask about your own life, so it does not give a Personal Utility curve. Adding the step values up, one after another, turns them into a curve that can be drawn on the same 0 to 1 scale as the others. This method was used with about 2,000 UK adults.

### Two Methods Side by Side

| | Choosing between gambles | Asking directly |
|---|---|---|
| What people do | Choose between a sure thing and a gamble | Rate how important each step is, from 0 to 100 |
| Gives Personal Utility? | Yes (choices about your own life) | No |
| Gives Societal Utility? | Yes (choices as a policymaker) | Yes |
| Number of people | About 300 | About 2,000 |

Both methods try to measure the same thing for society: how much a step up the Life Satisfaction scale is worth, and whether steps near the bottom are worth more than steps near the top. If they measure the same thing, you might expect them to give the same answer. A later page, [When Methods Disagree](/wiki/when-methods-disagree), looks at whether they do.
