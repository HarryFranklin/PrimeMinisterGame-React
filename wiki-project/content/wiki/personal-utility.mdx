**Personal Utility** measures how a person values changes in their own Life Satisfaction — the diminishing-returns curve from the previous page, applied specifically to decisions someone makes about their own life. It captures **risk aversion**: because an extra point of satisfaction is worth less to someone the higher they already sit, people generally become more cautious about their own outcomes the more they have to lose.

| Life Satisfaction | Personal Utility |
| --- | --- |
| 2 | 0.00 |
| 4 | 0.55 |
| 6 | 0.78 |
| 8 | 0.91 |
| 10 | 1.00 |

An intervention that lifts a struggling person from 2 to 4 delivers 0.55 of the total possible utility gain; the identical +2 intervention given to someone already at 8 delivers only 0.09 (0.91 to 1.00). Same policy, same fixed amount received — very different value to the two people receiving it.

> **Side Note: Education and Aspirations**  
> Interventions like higher education increase a person's opportunities, but they can also raise their aspirations. If reality doesn't keep pace with these higher standards, highly educated individuals can end up reporting lower Life Satisfaction despite having objectively better lives — a reminder that Personal Utility is about a person's own evaluation of their circumstances, not just their circumstances on paper.

### What Personal Utility Doesn't Capture
Personal Utility tells you how an individual would weigh outcomes for themselves — but people don't only make decisions about their own lives. Governments constantly make decisions *on behalf of* others: how to distribute a fixed budget, whose Life Satisfaction to prioritise, which group bears a cost. Personal Utility, built entirely around one person's own risk tolerance, has nothing to say about how to weigh one person's gain against another person's loss. That's a different kind of judgement — one this wiki calls **Societal Utility**.
