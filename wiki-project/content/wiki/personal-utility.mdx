**Personal Utility** measures how a person values changes in their *own* Life Satisfaction. It uses the diminishing-returns curve from the last page, applied to decisions that someone makes about their own life.

It also captures **risk aversion**: being careful about risks to your own wellbeing. The higher someone already sits on the scale, the less an extra point is worth to them. This means they have more to lose if a gamble goes wrong and less to gain if it pays off, which makes them less keen to take the risk in the first place.

A government that follows this idea tries to lift up people who are struggling, rather than adding to the lives of people who are already thriving. The measure it aims to raise is the **National Personal Satisfaction (NPS)**.

### What Personal Utility Doesn't Capture
Personal Utility shows how a person weighs outcomes for themselves. But people do not only make decisions about their own lives. Governments constantly make decisions on behalf of others, such as how to share a fixed budget, whose wellbeing to prioritise and which group has to bear a cost. Voters do this too, because when you vote on a policy you think about how it affects other people as well as yourself. That is a different kind of judgement, and it follows a different curve: **Societal Utility**. Where Personal Utility is about risk aversion, Societal Utility is about **inequality aversion**.
