**Personal Utility** measures how a person values changes in their own Life Satisfaction — the diminishing-returns curve from the previous page, applied specifically to decisions someone makes about their own life. It captures **risk aversion**: an extra point of satisfaction is worth less to someone the higher they already sit, so they have more to lose from a gamble going wrong, and less to gain if it pays off — which makes them less keen to take the risk in the first place.

> **Side Note: Education and Aspirations**  
> Interventions like higher education increase a person's opportunities, but they can also raise their aspirations. If reality doesn't keep pace with these higher standards, highly educated individuals can end up reporting lower Life Satisfaction despite having objectively better lives — a reminder that Personal Utility is about a person's own evaluation of their circumstances, not just their circumstances on paper.

### What Personal Utility Doesn't Capture
Personal Utility tells you how an individual would weigh outcomes for themselves — but people don't only make decisions about their own lives. Governments constantly make decisions *on behalf of* others: how to distribute a fixed budget, whose Life Satisfaction to prioritise, which group bears a cost. And it isn't just governments — when an individual votes on a policy, they're also weighing up how it affects other people, not just themselves, because their choice affects others too. That's a different kind of judgement, guided by a different curve: **Societal Utility**.
