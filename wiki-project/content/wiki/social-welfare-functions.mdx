When judging public policy, a government has to decide how to balance the competing needs of its citizens. To do this in a mathematical way, economists use a **Social Welfare Function (SWF)**.

An SWF is a rule that combines everyone's wellbeing scores into a single measure of national success. This lets us rank one outcome as better or worse than another.

An SWF can be built to care about more than the average. Two societies might share the same mean (average) Life Satisfaction. An SWF could still rank one of them lower if its floor (minimum) is much lower.

There is no single agreed-upon SWF. Choosing one is an ethical and political decision, not just a mathematical one. It decides whether a government puts the most weight on growth, on equality, or on protecting people who are struggling. In practice it is not a single either-or choice. There is a spectrum of possible SWFs, from caring only about the total to caring only about the worst-off, with many positions in between.

Two traditional ways of doing this sit at opposite ends of that spectrum:

1. **[Benthamite Aggregation](/wiki/benthamite-aggregation):** aim for the highest total wellbeing (maximising the mean).
2. **[Rawlsian Justice](/wiki/rawlsian-framework):** aim for the highest wellbeing for the worst-off person (maximising the minimum).

Before we explore newer ideas, we need to understand how these two foundational approaches work and where each one falls short.
