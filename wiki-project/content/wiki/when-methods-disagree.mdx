Utility-based policy depends on having the right curve. The page [Where Utility Curves Come From](/wiki/where-utility-curves-come-from) described two ways to measure one: choosing between gambles, and asking directly. Both methods have been used to measure Societal Utility in the UK. They do not agree.

With gambles, going from 8 to 10 is worth only about 7, compared with 100 for going from 2 to 4. When people are asked directly, the same step is worth about 55. Both methods agree that steps near the bottom matter more. They disagree about *how much* more. The gamble method puts far more weight on the worst-off.

The table also shows Personal Utility from gambles. The direct method did not ask people about their own lives, so there is no direct-asking column for Personal Utility.

### Why Might They Disagree?
Researchers do not know for sure. Some possible reasons are:

- **Gambles bring in risk.** A gamble asks people to face a chance of something bad happening. Some of the caution in their answers may be about risk itself, and not only about how much each step is worth.
- **Very small chances are hard to judge.** The gambles use chances as small as 1 in a million. People find it hard to tell tiny chances apart, and often treat them as bigger than they really are.
- **The questions are hard.** In the direct-asking study, about 1 in 3 people said the questions made little or no sense to them. People who struggled most tended to give every step a similar value, which makes the curve straighter.
- **Different people.** Each study asked a different group of people.

### Why This Matters for Policy
A different curve can give a different verdict. Go back to the worked example on [Applying Utility to Policy](/wiki/applying-utility-to-policy). A small group moves down from 4 to 3, and two large groups each move up by 1 point. Here is the same policy judged with each Societal Utility curve.

With the gamble curve, the policy is a net loss. With the direct-asking curve, it is a net gain. It is the same policy, affecting the same people, judged on the same idea of fairness. The answer flips depending only on how the curve was measured.

### The Weakness
A utility curve can look like a precise, scientific fact. It is not. It is built from people's answers to questions, and **different ways of asking can produce different utility curves, even when they are trying to measure the same thing**. This is a real limitation of utility-based approaches. A government that uses utility has to choose a method, and that choice can decide which policy wins. Like the choice between Benthamite and Rawlsian thinking, it is partly a question of values, and not just of maths.
