The Benthamite and Rawlsian approaches both use the raw 0-10 Life Satisfaction score, and neither asks how much a point is really worth to the person who gets it. A rise from 2 to 3 counts the same as a rise from 8 to 9, simply because both are "1 point". In maths, counting every point as equal is called treating gains as **linear**.

Research suggests that many people do not value every point equally. Escaping real hardship may matter far more than adding a little extra comfort to an already good life.

This is where **utility** comes in. Utility is how much a person values being in a given situation. A score tells us where someone is on the scale. Utility tells us how much a move along the scale matters to them. Research suggests that for most people, utility follows a curve of **diminishing marginal utility**. This means each extra point of Life Satisfaction ("marginal" just means "extra") is worth a little less than the one before it.

<UtilityCurveDiagram />

The curve starts at a score of 2. That is the lowest situation the research asked people about, and very few people in the UK score lower. So we treat 2 as the starting point, with a utility of 0.

From there, utility climbs steeply at first (the *steep climb*), slows down, and then flattens out (the *flat top*). Going from 2 to 4 is worth about 0.55 of all the utility there is. Going from 8 to 10 is a step of exactly the same size, but it is worth only about 0.09. Use the buttons on the diagram to look at each part of the curve, or to compare those two equal steps side by side.

So *where* a gain happens matters as much as *how big* it is.

### A Shift in Perspective
Money makes this easier to see. Say someone on a low income receives an extra £100. It might decide whether they can heat their home all winter, which is a huge change. Now give the same £100 to someone who is already comfortable. They barely notice it, because it disappears into savings or spending they would have done anyway. The amount is identical, but what it does for their wellbeing is not.

Because a point is worth more at the bottom, a government that counts utility will naturally care more about the worst-off, without ever having to say so. Rawls reached a similar place through a fairness argument, while utility theory gets there by asking what a gain is actually worth.

### Deciding for Yourself or for Others
Diminishing marginal utility is the first big idea, and it is enough on its own to explain why a point is not always worth the same. But there is a second idea, which is about *whose* outcome is being judged. People think differently about risks to their own life than about how wellbeing should be shared among people they do not know.
