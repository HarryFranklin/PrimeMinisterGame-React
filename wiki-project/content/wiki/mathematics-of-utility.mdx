[Benthamite Aggregation](/wiki/benthamite-aggregation) and the [Rawlsian Approach](/wiki/rawlsian-framework) both act directly on the raw 0-10 Life Satisfaction number — neither one considers how much a given point on that scale is actually worth to the person experiencing it. Psychological and economic research suggests a different picture: human wellbeing follows a curve of **diminishing marginal utility** — each additional point of satisfaction is worth less than the one before it.

> **Side Note: Rawls and the Worst-Off, Revisited**  
> There's a reason the difference principle and diminishing marginal utility point in a similar direction. If an extra point of Life Satisfaction really is worth more to someone with less of it, then a policymaker who weights gains by utility — rather than by raw LS points — will naturally end up caring more about the worst-off, without ever having to say so explicitly. Rawls got to that conclusion through a fairness argument (the veil of ignorance); utility theory gets there through a claim about how much a given gain is actually worth. They're different routes to a similar place.

The curve is anchored at zero from a Life Satisfaction score of 2 downward. Scores in that range typically reflect genuine hardship — a person's most basic needs for security, health, or income aren't being met. There's essentially no cushion left to diminish from.

From there, utility climbs steeply at first, then flattens out. Going from 2 to 4 is worth roughly 0.55 of the total possible utility. Going from 8 to 10 — the same four-point range — is worth only about 0.09.

In short: where a gain happens on the scale matters as much as how big it is.

### A Shift In Perspective
Money is a useful way to make this concrete. A £100 windfall might mean the difference between heating a home through winter or not for someone on a low income — a huge shift in circumstances. The same £100, given to someone already financially comfortable, barely registers; it's absorbed into savings or spending they'd have done anyway. The number is identical. What it buys, in terms of actual wellbeing, is not.

### Decisions for Self versus Decisions on Behalf of Others
Diminishing marginal utility is the first key idea in utility theory, and it's enough on its own to fix the linearity problem. But it isn't the whole picture. The second key idea is about *whose* outcome is being evaluated: as the [veil of ignorance](/wiki/rawlsian-framework) hints at, people reason differently about risks to their own life than they do about how wellbeing should be shared out across other people they don't know the position of. The next two pages build a curve for each of these separately — one for how people weigh outcomes for **themselves**, and one for how they weigh outcomes **across society**.
