Benthamite Aggregation and the Rawlsian Approach both, in their usual applied form, treat the 0-10 Life Satisfaction scale as linear. But psychological and economic research points the other way: human wellbeing follows a curve of **diminishing marginal utility** — each additional point of satisfaction is worth less than the one before it.

<UtilityCurveDiagram />

The curve above is anchored at zero around a Life Satisfaction score of 2 or below. That's deliberate: scores in that range typically reflect genuine hardship, where a person's most basic needs for security, health, or income aren't being met — there's essentially no cushion left to diminish from. From there, utility climbs steeply at first (going from 2 to 4 is worth roughly 0.55 of the total possible utility) and flattens out later (going from 8 to 10 is worth only about 0.09) — the same four-point range, worth wildly different amounts depending on where it starts.

### A Shift In Perspective
Money is a useful way to make this concrete. A £100 windfall might mean the difference between heating a home through winter or not for someone on a low income — a huge shift in circumstances. The same £100, given to someone already financially comfortable, barely registers; it's absorbed into savings or spending they'd have done anyway. The number is identical. What it buys, in terms of actual wellbeing, is not.

### Decisions for Self versus Decisions on Behalf of Others
Diminishing marginal utility is the first key idea in utility theory, and it's enough on its own to fix the linearity problem. But it isn't the whole picture. The second key idea is about *whose* outcome is being evaluated: people reason differently about risks to their own life than they do about how wellbeing should be shared out across other people. The next two pages build a curve for each of these separately — one for how people weigh outcomes for **themselves**, and one for how they weigh outcomes **across society**.
