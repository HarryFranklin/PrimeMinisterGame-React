Let's test both curves on the same policy.

### A Worked Example
Take a policy that affects three groups differently. One small group is a little below average and takes a modest hit. Two much larger groups are already doing fine and get a modest boost.

<PolicyImpactExample />

Under Personal Utility, this policy comes out ahead. The gains of the two large groups add up to more than the small group's loss. Under Societal Utility, the same policy becomes a net loss. The group at the bottom counts for more because they are worse off. That extra weight is enough to outweigh the gains that looked fine a moment ago.

### Why They Diverge
Both curves agree that a point at the bottom is worth more than a point at the top. They disagree about *how much* more. Societal Utility comes from choices people make for others, where they are more cautious, so it puts more weight on the worst-off. Personal Utility comes from choices people make about their own lives, where they take more risks, so it puts less weight on the worst-off.

The two views differ most when a policy gives a small loss to people who are worse off and gains to people who are already comfortable. For example, a tax change might slightly reduce support for low-income households and cut taxes for everyone else. It can raise total Personal Utility and still count as a loss under Societal Utility.

### What You Prefer
So a government that uses utility faces a choice. Personal Utility respects the choices people make for their own lives. Societal Utility reflects what people endorse when they are responsible for others. By now you have seen how Benthamite, Rawlsian and utility-based approaches can judge the same policy differently. None of them is simply "correct". Which one you find most convincing is a question of values. After you finish reading, you will be asked which approach you would choose.
