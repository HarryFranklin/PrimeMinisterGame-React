Personal Utility and Societal Utility don't always agree. A policy can benefit an individual while lowering weighted societal wellbeing overall — and the same policy can look very different depending on which lens you apply.

### A Worked Example
Consider a policy that affects three groups in the population differently:

| Group | Share of Population | Life Satisfaction Change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Struggling | 20% | −2 |
| Moderate | 40% | +1 |
| Comfortable | 40% | +1 |

**Personal Utility logic** asks: *if I knew which group I was in, would I support this?* Most people in the Moderate and Comfortable groups would — they gain. Only the Struggling group loses out.

**Societal Utility logic** asks: *evaluated from behind a veil of ignorance, not knowing which group you'd land in, is this policy good for society as a whole?* Because losses at the bottom of the distribution are weighted more heavily than gains at the top, this same policy may look net-negative once inequality aversion is factored in.

### Why the Two Logics Diverge
The gap between these two judgments is largest when a policy trades off a small loss for the worst-off against a larger absolute gain for those already comfortable. This is precisely the situation where framework choice — Benthamite, Rawlsian, or a utility-weighted hybrid — matters most.
