Personal Utility and Societal Utility don't always agree on the same policy. Where they diverge is where the government's chosen approach actually matters most.

### A Worked Example
Consider a policy that affects three groups differently: a small group who are already a little below average and take a modest hit, and two much larger groups who are already doing fine and get a modest boost.

<PolicyImpactExample />

Judged by **Personal Utility**, this policy comes out ahead overall — the population-weighted total is positive, because the two large groups' gains outweigh the smaller group's loss. Judged by **Societal Utility**, the same policy flips to a net loss — the group at the bottom is weighted more heavily precisely because they're worse off, and that's enough to outweigh the same gains that looked fine a moment ago.

### Where This Shows Up in Practice
The gap between the two judgements is largest whenever a policy trades a small loss for people who are already worse-off against gains for people who are already comfortable — precisely the situation Benthamite and Rawlsian logic already disagree about, now with a utility-weighted lens on top. A tax change that mildly reduces support for low-income households while cutting taxes broadly for everyone else is a good real-world example: it can raise total Personal Utility, while still registering as a loss under Societal Utility.

This also plays out at the individual level, not just in the aggregate. Someone might be willing to accept a small personal loss if it means overall Societal Utility is higher — effectively trading off their own outcome against everyone else's. How much any one person is willing to make that trade differs from person to person: some weigh their own outcome much more heavily than society's, others the reverse.

### What You Prefer
By now you've seen how Benthamite, Rawlsian, and utility-weighted approaches can each judge the very same policy differently — and none of them is simply "correct". Which one you find most persuasive, and how much weight you personally put on your own outcome versus everyone else's, is a value judgement. Once you've seen how these ideas translate into budgeting decisions, you'll be asked which of these approaches you'd choose.
