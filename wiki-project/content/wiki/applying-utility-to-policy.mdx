Personal Utility and Societal Utility don't always agree on the same policy — and where they diverge is exactly where framework choice matters most.

### A Worked Example
Consider a policy that affects three groups differently:

| Group | Share of Population | Life Satisfaction Change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Struggling | 20% | 4 → 2 |
| Moderate | 40% | 6 → 7 |
| Comfortable | 40% | 8 → 9 |

Judged by **Personal Utility** — would each group support this if it applied to them? — 80% of the population gains, and only the Struggling group loses out. Judged by **Societal Utility**, the picture flips: because losses at the bottom (dropping from 4 to 2 costs 0.72 of the societal utility range, versus only 0.55 under Personal Utility) are weighted more heavily than gains at the top, this same policy can come out net-negative once inequality aversion is factored in — even though the Moderate and Comfortable groups' gains look almost identical under either lens.

### Where This Shows Up in Practice
The gap between the two judgements is largest whenever a policy trades a small loss for the worst-off against a larger gain for people who are already comfortable — precisely the situation Benthamite and Rawlsian logic already disagree about, now with a utility-weighted lens on top. A tax change that mildly reduces support for low-income households while cutting taxes broadly for everyone else is a good real-world example: it can raise both the mean and total Personal Utility, while still registering as a loss under Societal Utility.

### What You Prefer
By now you've seen how Benthamite, Rawlsian, and utility-weighted approaches can each judge the very same policy differently — and none of them is simply "correct". Which one you find most persuasive is a value judgement about what matters most: raising everyone's position, protecting the worst-off, or minimising the gap between them. Once you've seen how these ideas translate into budgeting decisions, you'll be asked which of these approaches you'd choose.
