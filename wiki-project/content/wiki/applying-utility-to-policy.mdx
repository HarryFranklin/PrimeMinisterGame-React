Let's test both curves on the same policy.

### A Worked Example
Take a policy that affects three groups differently. One small group is a little below average and takes a modest hit. Two much larger groups are already doing fine and get a modest boost.

<PolicyImpactExample />

Under Personal Utility, this policy comes out ahead. The gains of the two large groups add up to more than the small group's loss. Under Societal Utility, the same policy becomes a net loss. The group at the bottom counts for more because they are worse off. That extra weight is enough to outweigh the gains that looked fine a moment ago.

### Where This Shows Up in Practice
The two views differ most when a policy gives a small loss to people who are worse off and gains to people who are already comfortable. This is where Benthamite and Rawlsian thinking already disagree. Utility adds a new way of weighing it. For example, a tax change might slightly reduce support for low-income households and cut taxes for everyone else. It can raise total Personal Utility and still count as a loss under Societal Utility.

This also happens for one person. Someone might accept a small loss for themselves if society ends up better off. They are trading their own outcome against everyone else's. People differ in how willing they are to do this. Some care much more about their own outcome than about society's. Others care more about society's.

### What You Prefer
By now you have seen how Benthamite, Rawlsian and utility-based approaches can judge the same policy differently. None of them is simply "correct". Which one you find most convincing is a question of values. So is how much weight you give your own outcome compared with everyone else's. After you finish reading, you will be asked which approach you would choose.
