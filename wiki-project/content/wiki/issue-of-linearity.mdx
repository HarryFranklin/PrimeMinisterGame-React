title: "The Issue with Linearity"
description: "Why treating every point of Life Satisfaction as equal breaks both frameworks."

---

Benthamite Aggregation and the Rawlsian Approach look like opposites — one chases the average, the other protects the floor. But both are built on the same hidden assumption: that the 0-10 Life Satisfaction scale is **linear**. That is, they treat a jump from 2 to 3 as worth exactly the same as a jump from 8 to 9.

Human experience doesn't work that way. Escaping real hardship — going from barely coping to getting by — plausibly matters far more than gaining a bit of extra comfort on top of an already good life. A framework that can't tell these two kinds of gain apart will keep making the same category of mistake, however it aggregates the numbers.

> **Side Note: What Bentham and Rawls Actually Said**  
> This isn't quite a flaw either philosopher missed. Bentham's own writing on utility discussed diminishing marginal value — the idea that an extra unit of pleasure or income is worth less to someone who already has plenty. Rawls's difference principle already leans toward protecting the worst-off by design. The flaw is in the *simplified, operational* versions of these ideas used in policy: raw averages and raw floors calculated directly from the 0-10 scale, with no adjustment for how much a point is actually worth to the person receiving it.

To fix this, we need a way of converting a raw Life Satisfaction score into a number that reflects how much it's actually worth to someone — not just where it sits on a 0-10 scale. That number is **Utility**.
