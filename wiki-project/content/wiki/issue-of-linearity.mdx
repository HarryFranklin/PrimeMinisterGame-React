description: "Why neither framework asks how much a Life Satisfaction gain is actually worth."

Benthamite Aggregation and the Rawlsian Approach disagree about *what* to optimise — the average versus the floor. But they share a blind spot: neither one ever asks how much a given change in Life Satisfaction is actually worth to the person experiencing it. They both just take the raw 0-10 number at face value and act on it directly — Benthamite sums it, Rawlsian looks only at its lowest value. A jump from 2 to 3 gets treated as the same size of gain as a jump from 8 to 9, simply because both are "1 point" on the scale.

Human experience doesn't work that way. Escaping real hardship — going from barely coping to getting by — plausibly matters far more than gaining a bit of extra comfort on top of an already good life. A framework that can't tell these two kinds of gain apart will keep making the same category of mistake, however it aggregates the numbers.

> **Side Note: What Bentham and Rawls Actually Said**  
> Bentham's own writing on utility discussed diminishing marginal value — the idea that an extra unit of pleasure or income is worth less to someone who already has plenty. Rawls's difference principle already leans toward protecting the worst-off by design. Both philosophers were reaching for something like this. What's missing from the simplified, operational versions of their ideas used in real policy — raw averages and raw floors, taken straight from the 0-10 scale — is a proper number for how much a given point is actually worth.

That's what **Utility** gives us: a measure of the satisfaction or value a person derives from a given state — not just where that state sits on the 0-10 scale. Crucially, different people value the same rung of that ladder differently, and the same person values moving between different rungs differently too.
