The most straightforward approach to wellbeing policy is rooted in Benthamite Utilitarianism. The goal under this framework is simple: **maximise total happiness**.

Under this ideology, a government succeeds if **average** Life Satisfaction across the population rises.

Imagine a country where half the population is comfortably well-off and the other half is only just getting by. A policy that gives the comfortable half a further boost — a tax cut, say — can lift the national mean quite a lot, purely because that half started closer to the top of the scale where a small nudge still counts as a gain. By Benthamite logic, that's a clear win. It does this without moving the other half of the population even slightly.

### The Problem with Averages
While maximising the average is an efficient way to increase total wellbeing, it says nothing about how that wellbeing is shared out. It is entirely possible — as in the example above — to raise the national average by making the wealthiest or already-comfortable citizens happier, while the poorest are left exactly where they started, or even worse off. A pure Benthamite approach can therefore mask real, ongoing suffering: it has no way of distinguishing "everyone gained a little" from "a few people gained a lot while everyone else gained nothing."

Two countries can post the exact same mean Life Satisfaction and still be worlds apart. One might have a fairly narrow spread, with almost everyone clustered near that average — a floor not far below the mean, and low dispersion. The other might have a comfortable majority pulling the mean up while a smaller group sits far below it — a much lower floor and much higher dispersion. Benthamite logic can't tell these two countries apart; it only sees the average.

The deeper issue is that a Benthamite government never has to ask how the worst-off citizens are doing — only whether the average went up. That's precisely the gap the next framework is built to close.
