The most straightforward approach to wellbeing policy is rooted in Benthamite Utilitarianism. The goal under this framework is simple: **maximise total happiness**.

Under this ideology, a government succeeds if average Life Satisfaction across the population rises.

Picture a country of ten citizens with Life Satisfaction scores of 4, 4, 4, 4, 4, 6, 6, 6, 6, 6 — a mean of 5. A policy that raises the six highest scorers to 8 lifts the national mean to 6.8, a clear win by Benthamite logic. But it does this without moving the four lowest-scoring citizens even slightly.

### The Problem with Averages
While maximising the average is an efficient way to increase total wellbeing, it says nothing about how that wellbeing is shared out. It is entirely possible — as in the example above — to raise the national average by making the wealthiest or already-comfortable citizens happier, while the poorest are left exactly where they started. A pure Benthamite approach can therefore mask real, ongoing suffering: it has no way of distinguishing "everyone gained a little" from "a few people gained a lot while everyone else gained nothing."

The deeper issue is that a Benthamite government never has to ask how the worst-off citizens are doing — only whether the average went up. Two countries with identical mean Life Satisfaction can have very different floors, and Benthamite logic is indifferent between them. That's precisely the gap the next framework is built to close.
