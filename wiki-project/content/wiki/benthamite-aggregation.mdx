The simplest approach to wellbeing policy is called Benthamite Utilitarianism. It is named after the philosopher Jeremy Bentham. Its goal is easy to state: *make total happiness as high as possible*. Its strength is that it is simple: one number to track, with every person's score counting equally.

Under this approach, a government has done well if the average Life Satisfaction across the population goes up. We can call this the **National Average Life Satisfaction (NALS)**. The idea is often summed up as "a rising tide lifts all boats".

Imagine a country where half the people are comfortable (scoring 6 to 8) and half are just getting by (scoring 4 to 5). A policy raises the score of everyone in the comfortable half by 1 point. The national average goes up, so by Benthamite logic this is a clear win. The average rises whoever gets the gain, so it counts as a win even though the other half is no better off.

### What the Average Leaves Out
Go back to Town A and Town B from the last page. They had the same mean, so a Benthamite government would rate them as equal. It only looks at the average.

The average also cannot tell some policies apart. In Town B, suppose one policy lifts everyone who scores 8 up to 9, and another lifts everyone who scores 3 up to 4. If the two groups are the same size, the mean rises by the same amount either way. The first policy helps people who were already doing well. The second helps people who were struggling. Under Benthamite logic, they are equally good.

A Benthamite government never has to ask how the worst-off people are doing, only whether the average went up.
