---
title: "When Utility Shouldn't Lead"
description: "Where weighing up total wellbeing should not decide policy questions."
category: "6. Limitations & Evaluation"
order: 1
---
Utility thinking is a useful way to compare trade-offs. But many people believe that some questions should not be settled by adding up wellbeing. Three areas show why.

**Rights.** Some protections, like free speech, a fair trial and protection from discrimination, are treated as non-negotiable. This holds even if removing them would raise average wellbeing. A society that got a higher average by taking away a minority's rights would still have done something most people think is wrong.

**Criminal justice.** A purely utilitarian approach could justify punishing an innocent person if it stopped enough crime to raise total wellbeing. Most legal systems reject this. They are built on fairness to the individual and due process, not on totals.

**Consent and bodily autonomy.** Decisions about your own body are treated as yours to make. This includes medical treatment and reproductive choices. It holds even if a utility calculation points to a different "best" result for society.

### The Common Thread
In each case, utility reasoning is one input among several. It should not be the only one. Knowing *when* to be careful is part of judging policy well.
