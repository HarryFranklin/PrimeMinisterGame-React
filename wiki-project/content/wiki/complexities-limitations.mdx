While Subjective Wellbeing is a vast improvement over GDP, measuring human emotion introduces complexities that policymakers must account for to avoid data misinterpretation.

### The "Happy Peasant" Problem & Adaptation
Humans possess a remarkable ability to adapt to their circumstances (intrapsychic adaptation). Over time, individuals living in severe deprivation may adjust their expectations downwards, reporting relatively high life satisfaction. Conversely, wealthy individuals may adapt to luxury and report dissatisfaction. 

If policy is driven solely by self-reported scores, it risks over-rewarding the privileged "frustrated achievers" while ignoring the objective suffering of resilient, deprived communities.

### Cultural Variance
Different cultures attach different weights to positive and negative experiences. Western cultures tend to prioritise self-enhancement and positive affect, whereas Eastern cultures often prioritise social harmony and self-criticism, giving negative affect more weight in their subjective evaluations. 

These cultural differences can skew international or cross-demographic comparisons if not properly calibrated.

### When Utility Reasoning Shouldn't Lead

Utility-based frameworks are powerful for comparing trade-offs, but not every policy question should be settled by weighing aggregate wellbeing. Three domains illustrate why:

**Rights-based decisions.** Some protections — freedom of speech, due process, protection from discrimination — are treated as non-negotiable regardless of whether restricting them would raise average wellbeing. A society that could "average out" ahead by suppressing a minority's rights has still done something most people would consider wrong, no matter what the utility math says.

**Criminal justice and sentencing.** A purely utilitarian approach could justify punishing an innocent person if it deterred enough future crime to raise net societal utility. Most legal systems reject this outright: justice systems are built on individual desert and due process, not aggregate outcomes.

**Informed consent and bodily autonomy.** Decisions about an individual's own body — medical treatment, reproductive choices — are generally treated as belonging to that person, even where a utility calculation might suggest a different "optimal" outcome for society.

### The Common Thread

In each case, utility-based reasoning is treated as one input among several, rather than the deciding one. Recognising *when* a domain calls for this kind of caution — not just applying utility maximisation uniformly — is itself part of good policy evaluation.
