Let's start with Benthamite logic in practice. It shows up wherever a policy is judged a success if it raises the overall average — even when the gains aren't evenly spread. Common examples include:

- **Broad tax cuts**, which stimulate general economic activity and consumer spending across the income scale
- **Universal infrastructure investment** (roads, broadband, transport links) that raises productivity and convenience nationwide
- **Population-wide public health campaigns** (e.g. general anti-smoking advertising) aimed at everyone, rather than at high-risk groups specifically

### The Common Thread
Each of these policies is judged a success under exactly one condition: **does the national average Life Satisfaction rise?** Whether the gains are concentrated among people who were already comfortable, or spread evenly across the population, doesn't factor into the verdict.

> **Side Note: The Easterlin Paradox**  
> Why don't societies necessarily get happier as they get richer? The Easterlin Paradox is the observation that, past a certain point, rising national income doesn't reliably translate into rising average Life Satisfaction — partly because people judge their circumstances against a shifting reference point (what their neighbours have, what they're used to) rather than in absolute terms. It's a reminder that even a policy which successfully raises the average by conventional economic measures may do less for wellbeing than it looks like on paper.
