Now let's look at Rawlsian thinking in practice. It shows up when a policy is aimed at raising the minimum, even at some cost to people who are already doing well. Common examples include:

- **Means-tested welfare payments**, paid only to people below a set level of hardship
- **A Universal Credit uplift**, which gives more money to the poorest households and is paid for with higher taxes on middle and high earners
- **Minimum wage laws**, which lift the lowest wages without directly affecting high earners
- **Targeted social housing**, paid for by progressive taxes and given to the most vulnerable people

<RawlsianPolicyEffects/>

Take the same country as the Benthamite example: a comfortable majority and a struggling minority. A Rawlsian policy aims at the struggling minority. It barely moves the national average, so by Benthamite logic it looks weak. But it does something the Benthamite policy never did. It lifts the worst-off people off the floor.

### The Common Thread
Each of these policies is a success if the **floor** rises. Where the average ends up matters less. So does how much better off comfortable people become. Sometimes the policy comes at their expense.

So far you have seen two clear but opposite answers to "what should a policy achieve?": raise the average, or raise the floor. Both work directly from the raw 0-10 score. The next page asks what a single point on that scale is really worth to different people.
