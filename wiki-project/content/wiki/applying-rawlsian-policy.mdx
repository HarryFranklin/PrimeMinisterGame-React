Rawlsian reasoning shows up wherever a policy is explicitly targeted at raising the minimum — even at some cost to those who are already doing well. Common examples include:

- **Means-tested welfare payments**, directed only at those below a defined poverty or hardship threshold
- **Minimum wage legislation**, which raises the wage floor without directly affecting high earners
- **Targeted social housing**, funded through progressive taxation and allocated to the most vulnerable

Picture the same ten-citizen population from the Benthamite example, scored 4, 4, 4, 4, 4, 6, 6, 6, 6, 6 — a floor of 4. A Rawlsian policy that raises just the five lowest scorers to 5 barely moves the national mean (from 5.0 to 5.5), and by Benthamite logic looks like a fairly weak result. But it has done something the Benthamite policy never did: it has lifted the worst-off citizens off the floor.

### The Common Thread
Each of these policies is judged a success if the **floor** rises. Where the average ends up, or how much better off already-comfortable citizens become, is secondary — sometimes explicitly at their expense.

> **Side Note: The Trade-off**  
> Rawlsian policies can look expensive relative to their effect on the national average, since resources are deliberately concentrated on a smaller, worse-off group rather than spread broadly. A Benthamite evaluation and a Rawlsian evaluation of the same policy can therefore reach opposite conclusions about whether it was worth the cost.

Between them, Benthamite and Rawlsian policy give you two clean, opposite answers to "what should this policy achieve": raise the average, or raise the floor. But both are doing the same thing mathematically underneath — treating every point of Life Satisfaction gained as worth exactly the same, wherever it happens to fall on the 0-10 scale. That assumption turns out to be the weak point in both frameworks.
