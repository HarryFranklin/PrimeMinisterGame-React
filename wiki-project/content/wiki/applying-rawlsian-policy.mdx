Now let's look at Rawlsian logic in practice. It shows up wherever a policy is explicitly targeted at raising the minimum — even at some cost to those who are already doing well. Common examples include:

- **Means-tested welfare payments**, directed only at those below a defined poverty or hardship threshold
- **Minimum wage legislation**, which raises the wage floor without directly affecting high earners
- **Targeted social housing**, funded through progressive taxation and allocated to the most vulnerable

<RawlsianPolicyEffects/>

Take the same country from the Benthamite example — comfortable majority, struggling minority. A Rawlsian policy that specifically targets the struggling minority barely moves the national mean, and by Benthamite logic looks like a fairly weak result. But it has done something the Benthamite policy never did: it has lifted the worst-off citizens off the floor.

### The Common Thread
Each of these policies is judged a success if the **floor** rises. Where the average ends up, or how much better off already-comfortable citizens become, is secondary — sometimes explicitly at their expense.

> **Side Note: The Trade-off**  
> Rawlsian policies can look expensive relative to their effect on the national average, since resources are deliberately concentrated on a smaller, worse-off group rather than spread broadly. But research on wellbeing suggests that people moving out of a genuinely low state tend to value that improvement more than someone starting from a higher position would value the identical numerical gain — simply because of how much worse things were before. A Benthamite evaluation and a Rawlsian evaluation of the same policy can therefore reach very different conclusions about whether it was worth the cost.

Between them, Benthamite and Rawlsian policy give you two clean, opposite answers to "what should this policy achieve": raise the average, or raise the floor. But neither one asks how much a given Life Satisfaction gain is actually *worth* to the person receiving it — they just look at the raw number moving. That gap is what we'll look at next.
